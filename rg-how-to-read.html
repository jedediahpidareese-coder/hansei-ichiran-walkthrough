35  How to read these documents

The documents in this book were written in the 1860s–1880s in the formal written language of the Meiji state. That language is not the modern Japanese of the JLPT. Three things stand between an N2 reader and the page.

35.1 The three walls

1. The kanji look wrong — 旧字体 (old character forms). Before the 1946 spelling reform, many kanji were written in fuller forms: 廢 for 廃, 縣 for 県, 爲 for 為, 舊 for 旧, 藏 for 蔵, 圓 for 円. You already know the modern character; you just have to recognise its older shape. The old-kanji primer tabulates every form that appears in these decrees.

2. The verb endings are in katakana — カタカナ送り仮名. Meiji official prose writes the kanji of a word normally but puts its inflections and the grammatical particles in katakana: 廢シテ, 爲ス, セシム, ヲ, ノ. This looks alien but is purely a spelling convention. Read シテ as して, ヲ as を, ノ as の, and the katakana melts away.

3. The grammar is classical — 文語 / 漢文訓読体. This is the real wall, because the JLPT tests only modern grammar. The decrees use the classical auxiliary-verb system — the causative しむ (= modern させる), the past き (連体形 し), the negative ず／ざる, the obligation/conjecture べし, the copulas なり／たり — together with a Sino-Japanese word order inherited from reading classical Chinese. The grammar primer walks through each form and bridges it to the modern equivalent you already command.

A fourth, lower wall is vocabulary: the Meiji bureaucratic and legal lexicon (詔, 勅, 版籍奉還, 秩禄, 華族, 太政官…), covered in its own primer, and the older numeral, date, and unit systems, covered here.

35.2 The four-layer method

Every worked passage is presented in four aligned layers, so you can climb from the raw page down to plain sense one rung at a time:

Layer What it is Why it’s there
① 原文 the text verbatim as printed — old kanji, katakana okurigana, original punctuation this is what you are actually trying to read; everything else is scaffolding
② 書き下し the same words, same order, rewritten in modern script (新字体 + 現代仮名遣い) removes walls 1 and 2 so only the grammar remains
③ 現代語訳 natural modern Japanese removes wall 3 — shows what the sentence means
④ English a faithful English rendering the claim you are auditing

Hover (or tap) any dotted word in the 原文 for its reading, its modern equivalent, and a short grammar note. Buttons above each passage let you hide the furigana, the modern-Japanese layer, or the English — so you can test yourself by covering the lower rungs and climbing back up.

Here is the method on a single famous line — the core of the 1871 abolition decree:

Show / hide:
原文
列藩ヲ廢廢シテ → 廃して（はいして）abolish旧字体 廢 → 新字体 廃。文語サ変「廢す」＝ 現代「廃する」。シテ＝して。シテ悉クことごとくevery one, without exception副詞。布告・詔勅に頻出。縣けんprefecture旧字体 縣 → 新字体 県。ト爲スなすmake (X) into (Y)旧字体 爲 → 為。「A ヲ B ト爲ス」＝ A を B にする。終止形 爲ス（す）。。
書き下し
列藩れっぱんを廃はいして悉ことごとく県けんと為なす。
現代語訳
すべての藩を廃止し、ことごとく県にする。
English
Abolish all the domains and make them, every one, into prefectures.

Reading the 原文 aloud in modern kana — reppan wo haishite kotogotoku ken to nasu — you can already hear that it is close to modern Japanese. The distance is small and learnable, and closing it is the whole purpose of this section.

35.3 A note on direction and the scan

The originals are printed vertically, read top-to-bottom and right-to-left; a two-page spread is read right page first. This guide reproduces the text horizontally for the web, but where it matters — and in the primary-source scans — the original orientation is preserved. When a character is genuinely hard to make out in the scan, the guide flags it as uncertain rather than guessing; the surest check is always the image itself.
