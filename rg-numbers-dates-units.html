39  Numbers, dates & units

Meiji documents count, date, and measure in systems that N2 study never touches. None of it is hard, but all of it is unfamiliar, and it is where a translation most often goes quietly wrong. This primer covers the three you need: kanji numerals, the 和暦 calendar, and the 石・両・貫 measurement systems.

39.1 Kanji numerals

Numbers are written with characters, place-value style:

一 1 二 2 三 3 四 4 五 5
六 6 七 7 八 8 九 9 十 10
百 100 千 1,000 萬／万 10,000 〇／零 0

Values are built by multiplication-then-addition, largest unit first: 五百四十 = 5×100 + 40 = 540; 六百七拾五 = 675. Watch for three things:

  • 大字 (formal numerals). To prevent tampering, legal and fiscal texts often use elaborated forms: 壹 = 一, 貳 = 二, 參 = 三, 拾 = 十. So 貳千六百貳拾石 = 二千六百二十石 = 2,620 koku.
  • Fractions read right-to-left with 分. 十分之一 = “of ten parts, one” = 1/10. 四分之一 = 1/4. 十分一之半 = “half of the one-tenth” = 1/20.
  • 萬 is 10,000, not “million.” 百萬石 = 100 × 10,000 = 1,000,000 koku. Domain sizes are almost always quoted in 萬石 (man-goku) units: 五十四萬石 = 540,000.

39.2 Dates — the 和暦 calendar

A Meiji date has the form 元号 + year + month + day, e.g. 明治四年七月十四日 = Meiji 4, month 7, day 14.

Converting the year: Meiji 1 = 1868, so Gregorian year ≈ 1867 + Meiji year. Meiji 4 = 1871. (Taishō 1 = 1912; Shōwa 1 = 1926 — the same trick with a different base.)

But the month and day are lunisolar, not Gregorian, until the calendar switch at the end of 1872. So 明治四年七月十四日 is not “14 July 1871” — it is 29 August 1871. You cannot convert a pre-1873 month/day just by keeping the number; the lunar date runs roughly a month behind, and some years insert an extra 閏月 (intercalary month).

Two anchors make this manageable for the documents here:

  • The abolition decree, 明治4年7月14日 = 29 August 1871, is the fixed point everything else in 1871 is measured against.
  • Meiji 3 inserts an intercalary 10th month (閏十月); Meiji 4’s intercalary month falls in the 10th, after the abolition. Ignoring the 閏 month throws conversions off by ~30 days — a mistake worth knowing about because an earlier draft of the debt-petition dataset in this project made exactly it.

The 干支 (sexagenary) year. Documents often name the year by its zodiac-stem instead of the era: 庚午 (kanoe-uma) = Meiji 3 = 1870, 辛未 (kanoto-hitsuji) = Meiji 4 = 1871. A petition dated 「庚午九月五日」 is Meiji 3/9/5. The debt-petition chapter reads one of these dates in context.

干支 Era year Gregorian
庚午 Meiji 3 1870
辛未 Meiji 4 1871
壬申 Meiji 5 1872

39.3 Units — rice, land, money

石高 (kokudaka): the rice-yield system. Domains were measured not in area or population but in 石 (koku) of assessed rice yield — the currency of the whole Tokugawa order. The volume ladder, each unit 1/10 of the one before:

\[\text{石 (こく)} \;>\; \text{斗 (と)} \;>\; \text{升 (しょう)} \;>\; \text{合 (ごう)} \;>\; \text{勺 (しゃく)} \;>\; \text{才／夕 (さい)}\]

So 六百七拾五石六斗八升一合六夕七才 is a precise yield of about 675.6817 koku. You will also meet three related assessments the dataset keeps distinct:

Term Reading What it measures
草高 くさだか nominal / gross assessed yield (the “official” size)
表高 おもてだか the formally registered kokudaka (rank basis)
現米 げんまい actual rice actually collected as revenue

Money. The pre-Meiji and early-Meiji coinage: 両 (ryō), subdivided into 分 and 朱; from 1871 the new 圓／円 (yen), subdivided 錢 (sen, 1/100) and 厘 (rin, 1/1000). Domain stipends are often quoted in 俵 (hyō, bales) — roughly 0.4 koku each — rather than in koku.

Weight and han-note terms, met occasionally: 貫 (kan) and 匁 (monme) for weight and for silver money; 石 again for the han’s paper bills (藩札) reckoned against rice.

TipWhy the units matter for reading translations

A great many translation errors are unit errors: reading 萬 as “million,” dropping the 閏 month, or silently turning a lunar date into a Gregorian one. When you check an English rendering against the 原文, check the numbers and dates first — they are the easiest thing to verify and the easiest thing to get wrong.
